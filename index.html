ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE USE POLICY

1. Purpose, Scope and Principles

This policy lets the firm use generative AI (large language models, AI research assistants, drafting, transcription and summarisation tools, and AI features embedded in other software) to serve clients better, while meeting our duties of competence, confidentiality, candour to the tribunal, supervision and honest billing.

a)      Lawyer accountable. AI is an assistant, never the decision-maker. The responsible lawyer owns every output they use.

b)      Confidentiality first. Client information goes only into tools approved for its sensitivity.

c)      Verify before relying. No AI output reaches a client, court, counterparty or regulator unchecked.

d)     Transparency. Clients and tribunals are told about AI use when required by law, contract or good judgment.

e)      Proportionality. Controls scale with risk: more sensitive data and higher stakes mean more review.

Approval Criteria for New Tools

1.      Submit a request to the Committee describing the use case, data and users.

2.      Security and privacy review: vendor certifications (for example SOC 2 Type II or ISO 27001), sub-processors, breach history, retention and deletion, model-training terms.

3.      Legal review of contract terms: confidentiality, indemnities, IP ownership of outputs, privilege preservation.

4.      Pilot with a small group, then Register entry with tier, permitted data, permitted uses and an owner.

Users must not connect AI tools to firm email, document systems or matter files unless the integration is itself approved.

6. Verification Steps

AI tools can produce confident but wrong or invented material, including non-existent cases, misquoted statutes and plausible but incorrect reasoning. The lawyer using the output must complete every step below that applies, and record completion in the matter file.

1.      Source check. Locate every cited case, statute, regulation, rule and secondary source in an authoritative database. Confirm it exists, the citation is correct and the court or issuing body is right.

2.      Quote and holding check. Read the actual passage. Confirm quotations are verbatim and that the authority really supports the proposition stated.

3.      Currency check. Run a citator or equivalent to confirm the authority is still good law and has not been overruled, amended or repealed.

4.      Jurisdiction and facts check. Confirm the law is from the right jurisdiction and date, and that the output matches the matter’s documents. Check names, dates, figures, defined terms and cross-references.

5.      Reasoning check. Independently test the analysis. Ask whether you would reach the same conclusion without the tool, and look for missing counterarguments or unstated assumptions.

6.      Completeness and bias check. For review or summarisation, sample the underlying documents to confirm nothing material was omitted, and check outputs for discriminatory or skewed framing.

7.      Confidentiality check. Make sure the output does not leak other clients’ information and that no unintended data was pasted back in.

8.      Sign-off and record. The responsible lawyer approves the final work product and logs the tool used, purpose, date and verification performed. Higher-risk work (filings, opinions) needs a second reviewer.

Court rules. Before any filing, check whether the court or tribunal has a standing order or rule on AI use or certification, and comply with it exactly. Where a certification is required, only a lawyer who personally completed the verification steps may sign.

7. Client Disclosure and Consent

Baseline

a)      Engagement letters include a standard AI clause explaining that the firm may use approved AI tools, that those tools are vetted for confidentiality, and that lawyers remain responsible for all work.

b)      Outside-counsel guidelines. Intake must capture any client terms on AI. Where a client prohibits or restricts AI, that term overrides this policy and is flagged on the matter record.

8. Supervision, Fees and Conflicts

        i.            Supervision. Partners and supervising lawyers are responsible for ensuring that associates, paralegals and staff follow this policy and that AI-assisted work is reviewed to the same standard as human work.

      ii.            Billing. Bill for the time actually spent, including review and verification. Do not bill hourly for time saved by AI as though it were lawyer time. Pass tool costs to clients only if disclosed in the engagement terms and permitted by law. Flat-fee and value-based arrangements may reflect efficiency gains.

    iii.            Intellectual property. Do not enter third-party copyrighted or licensed material into tools where terms forbid it. Check ownership and licence terms for generated content before delivering it as client work.

    iv.            Conflicts. Do not use any tool that could expose one client’s information to another. Report any suspected cross-matter leakage immediately.

      v.            Bias and fairness. Do not use AI outputs that could unlawfully discriminate, including in hiring, lending or eligibility analysis, without review by Legal.

9. Incidents and Breaches

Report within 24 hours to the CISO and General Counsel if you: enter data into an unapproved tool or the wrong tier; suspect leakage of confidential data; submit or circulate unverified or fabricated AI content; or notice a tool behaving unexpectedly.

1.      Contain: stop use, preserve evidence, request deletion from the vendor where possible.

2.      Assess: data involved, clients affected, privilege and regulatory impact.

3.      Notify: clients, courts and regulators as required by law, contract and professional rules, with the Risk Partner deciding timing and content. Correct any inaccurate filing promptly.

4.      Remediate and learn: root-cause review and policy or training updates.

Good-faith early reporting will be treated favourably. Concealment is a disciplinary matter.

10. Training, Monitoring and Enforcement

        i.            Training. Mandatory induction on this policy before tool access, annual refreshers, and role-specific sessions on verification and prompt hygiene.

      ii.            Logging and audit. Tier 1 tools keep usage logs. Information Security and Risk run quarterly sampling of matters for compliance with data tiers and verification records.

    iii.            Register upkeep. The Committee reviews every tool at least annually, and immediately after a vendor terms change or security event.

    iv.            Enforcement. Breaches may lead to loss of tool access, retraining, formal discipline up to dismissal, and referral to a regulator where required.

      v.            Exceptions. Only the General Counsel may grant written, time-limited exceptions.
